About me

Designing technology to restore independence and enhance the human experience.

Hello, and welcome to my digital portfolio! I am Kushagra Dashora, a Senior pursuing my Bachelor's Degree in Robotics Engineering at Arizona State University, with an expected graduation date of May 2026. My college journey has been profoundly shaped by my participation in the National Academy of Engineering's Grand Challenges Scholars Program (GCSP). Before joining this program, my perspective on engineering was purely technical. However, GCSP has challenged me to view problem-solving through a holistic, human-centered lens.

My work and this portfolio are guided by the GCSP theme "Joy of Living." To me, this theme represents a lifelong commitment to developing technologies that actively enhance quality of life and restore independence, particularly for individuals facing physical and mobility challenges.

As you scroll through this portfolio, you will find a curated narrative of my collegiate experiences spanning the five core GCSP competencies: Talent, Multidisciplinary, Entrepreneurship, Multicultural, and Social Consciousness. You will see how my journey evolved from researching automated manufacturing to developing affordable prosthetics and leading campus tours. I encourage you to explore these pages to understand how these interconnected experiences have prepared me to tackle the grand challenges of our future.

Talent Competency

Research & Creative Project Experience

Capstone Project: Wisk Aero Partnership

Automated Battery Sorting System

During my senior year (Fall 2025 to Spring 2026), I engaged in a comprehensive capstone research project collaborating with Wisk Aero, a pioneering company in autonomous electric aviation. Instead of working on theoretical problems, my team was tasked with solving a highly practical manufacturing hurdle: designing an innovative Automated Battery Sorting System for their Generation 6 electric VTOL aircraft assembly line. The existing factory automation required manual separation of pouch cells based on embedded QR code data, a process that was too slow to meet Wisk's aggressive production demands over the next three years.

Working as the Team Lead, I was responsible for guiding our multidisciplinary team through complex timelines while personally focusing on the technical systems integration. I spearheaded the integration of a Siemens PLC to create a fully remote, Ethernet-based controlled factory system. By applying this Internet of Things (IoT) approach, I successfully connected the hardware directly through the network. This not only removed the manual sorting bottleneck but made the entire robotic cell space vastly more efficient, accessible, and ready to scale.

Value and Connection to Theme

This hands-on research experience was invaluable for my professional career. It bridged the gap between theoretical robotics learned in the classroom and practical, scalable manufacturing used in the real world. It taught me the critical role of IoT in modern manufacturing and how to manage complex stakeholder expectations in the aerospace industry.

Furthermore, this project directly aligns with my Joy of Living theme. By optimizing the manufacturing systems for electric VTOLs, I am contributing to the advancement of sustainable, electric aviation technology. These aircraft represent a cleaner, more accessible transportation solution that will ultimately enhance quality of life by reducing travel time, decreasing environmental impact, and radically improving urban mobility.

Multidisciplinary Competency

Interdisciplinary Learning

FSE 150: Introduction to Grand Challenges

During my freshman year in Fall 2022, FSE 150 served as my gateway into the GCSP. This course fundamentally shifted my mindset, teaching me to understand how engineering solutions have wide-ranging, interdisciplinary implications on society. We spent the semester exploring the historical context and cultural impacts of emerging technologies, moving far beyond mere technical specifications.

A particularly meaningful assignment for me was our team project. My team pitched the concept of a personalized exosuit, an idea heavily inspired by the realistic mechanical capabilities seen in sci-fi media like Iron Man. We envisioned and designed a theoretical suit intended to make daily physical labor easier, faster, and safer for the working class. This exercise challenged me to think beyond current technological constraints and actively envision the long-term societal impacts of emerging technologies, laying a strong ethical framework for all my future designs.

HST 318: History of Engineering

In Spring 2026, I completed HST 318, a transformative course that encouraged me to develop a true systems mindset. We transcended technical boundaries to investigate how engineering historically intersects with policy, human behavior, and economics. For my final project, I wrote a comprehensive essay titled "Pragmatic Art," analyzing Bill Hammack's book The Things We Make.

Before this class, I held the belief that engineering was strictly applied science. However, reading about how medieval cathedral builders relied heavily on heuristics, geometry, and rules of thumb rather than absolute scientific truths completely shifted my perspective. I learned that engineering is fundamentally an iterative process of trial, error, and optimizing competing constraints—much like how my teams have had to iteratively adjust physical hardware after sensors failed in the lab. This realization was incredibly liberating for my academic career; it taught me to abandon the pressure of theoretical perfection and instead embrace failure as a necessary tool for innovation.

Value & Connection to GCSP Theme

Both of these interdisciplinary courses have been essential in shaping my approach to the Joy of Living theme. I learned that creating technologies to enhance human life isn't just a math problem—it requires an understanding of historical context, societal impact, and ethical consequences. This holistic viewpoint makes me a far more responsible robotics engineer, capable of designing solutions that truly serve communities without causing unintended harm.

Entrepreneurship Competency

Business Viability & Innovation

FSE 301: Entrepreneurship and Value Creation

In Fall 2024, I took FSE 301, which became a pivotal turning point in my academic journey. It provided the exact framework I needed to take a technical team project—Devils Prosthetics—and transform it into a viable business venture. Devils Prosthetics is an initiative aimed at creating low-cost, 3D-printed myoelectric arms for trans-radial amputees. With standard prosthetics costing anywhere from $5,000 to over $100,000, our goal was to bring the cost down to a fraction of that price, making them accessible to households facing insurance coverage gaps.

Throughout the course, my role evolved from purely focusing on circuitry and engineering to understanding market viability and customer discovery. I practiced active listening and empathy by engaging directly with potential prosthetic users, integrating their diverse functional and aesthetic needs into our business models. I led the development of our Evidence-Based Pitch Deck, where we established a tiered product line—ranging from a $700 "BudgetBionic" model to a $3,000 "BionicsRewired" model. This tiered structure ensured our technology could bypass traditional insurance barriers and reach various economic demographics.

This course was incredibly valuable to my professional career because it taught me how to communicate complex technical visions to non-technical stakeholders. By outlining our value proposition, market differentiation, and an actionable IRB approval roadmap for future human testing, our team successfully secured $10,000 in EPICS Elite Pitch Funding. This entrepreneurial experience directly embodies my Joy of Living theme by proving that an engineering solution only restores independence and brings joy to people if it is financially accessible and business-viable enough to actually reach the market.

Multicultural Competency

Global Perspectives

Engineering for the Joy of Living means engineering for a diverse, global population. For my work in robotics and prosthetics, cultural awareness is essential. Different cultures have varying attitudes toward assistive technologies, aesthetic preferences, and unique functional requirements based on their daily traditions. My coursework in ASB 300 and SLC 212 equipped me with this critical multicultural perspective.

ASB 300: Food and Culture

During the Spring 2025 semester, ASB 300 offered me a fascinating lens into how food serves as a foundation for understanding diverse social structures, economic values, and cultural traditions. We examined how globalization impacts food distribution and how traditions reflect deep-seated community values.

For one of my primary assignments, I conducted a participant observation at Infusion Coffee & Tea in Tempe. I spent hours analyzing how the shop acts as a "third place"—a concept separate from home and work where community engagement and the exchange of cultural capital flourish. By studying food and social environments as cultural artifacts, I developed a deeper sensitivity to the varying ways quality of life is defined, socialized, and nurtured across different community contexts.

SLC 212: Languages and Cultures Clash

In the Fall 2025 semester, SLC 212 provided an in-depth examination of how language shapes cultural identity and how miscommunications can lead to conflict in diverse, professional settings. We explored various historical and contemporary case studies of cultural clashes in the workplace.

I practically applied these course concepts by interviewing Professor Pavan Pillalamari, a physics professor from India, to analyze intercultural miscommunication in mainstream American academia. We discussed the friction caused by "Anglo scripts," such as navigating indirect feedback loops, deciphering silence as a sign of respect versus disengagement, and recognizing superficial social invitations. This project was highly valuable, teaching me vital strategies for communicating across boundaries and mitigating friction on international engineering teams.

Social Consciousness Competency

Service Learning

EPICS: Devils Prosthetics

From Fall 2023 to Spring 2024, I fulfilled a major portion of my service learning through the Engineering Projects in Community Service (EPICS) program. EPICS is an award-winning social entrepreneurship program where student teams design, build, and deploy systems to solve engineering-based problems for local charities and communities. Serving as the Electrical Design Lead for Devils Prosthetics, I focused on developing an affordable, 3D-printed myoelectric prosthetic for children facing limb loss.

Working alongside a diverse team of mechanical engineers, computer scientists, and fellow robotics students, I took charge of designing the servo controller system and assisting with the 3D printed mechanical models. We worked directly with our community partner, Noah Nemgar, CPO at Limb Labs, to ensure our prosthetic met real-world patient needs. This experience went far beyond technical execution—we grew so dedicated to our mission of social consciousness that we successfully navigated the legal and operational steps to transition our student project into a registered non-profit LLC. Witnessing the tangible impact our prosthetic could have on an individual's independence deeply underscored the significance of social responsibility in engineering.

Fulton Ambassadors

My second major service commitment was bridging communities as a Fulton Ambassador, a vital organization that links prospective students, donors, and the broader public to the vibrant engineering community within Arizona State University. Spanning three and a half years of my college life—from Fall 2022 to Spring 2026—I evolved into high-level leadership roles, serving as both the Poly Co-Tour Director and the Polytechnic Campus President.

In these roles, I was the human face of our engineering program. I gave countless campus tours, connected with Fulton donors at official events, and mentored high schoolers, continuing to guide many of them even after they enrolled as college students. Helping young students realize their potential in STEM was incredibly fulfilling. For my dedication to representing the school and leaving a lasting impact, I was honored to receive both the Rookie of the Year and Best Legacy awards.

Value & Connection to Theme: Both my time in EPICS and as a Fulton Ambassador directly embody my Joy of Living theme. These experiences shifted my perspective from simply building robots in a vacuum to engaging deeply with the community. They proved to me that the true purpose of engineering is not just technological advancement, but human connection and dedicated service.

Concluding Remarks

Becoming a Grand Challenges Scholar

My journey through the Grand Challenges Scholars Program has been a transformative experience that seamlessly weaves together diverse disciplines under the unifying theme of the Joy of Living. When I started my engineering education, my focus was largely confined to technical execution—I wanted to know how to code, solder, and build. However, participating in GCSP required me to step back and view engineering as a multidimensional tool for human betterment. The connectivity between the five competencies has been the most profound realization of my college career.

This connectivity is evident in how each competency built upon the others. My talent competency—researching automated manufacturing and IoT integration for Wisk Aero—gave me the hard technical skills necessary to build complex systems. But it was my multidisciplinary coursework in history and science fiction that taught me to view those technical systems through an ethical lens, ensuring my designs are responsible and grounded in reality. When I took those technical skills into the service learning environment of EPICS to build prosthetics, my multicultural understanding ensured I was designing with cultural sensitivity and empathy for the end user. Finally, my entrepreneurial training provided the business acumen necessary to actually bring those prosthetics out of the lab, secure funding, and navigate the transition into a non-profit LLC.

Together, these experiences have prepared me exceptionally well for my future career. I am leaving Arizona State University not just knowing how to program a Siemens PLC or build a robotic arm, but knowing how to lead multidisciplinary teams, communicate effectively with global stakeholders, and center the human experience in my designs. The GCSP has transformed me from a student focused primarily on technical skills into a well-rounded robotics engineer prepared to tackle humanity's greatest challenges.

Overall, the Grand Challenges Scholars Program has been the defining pillar of my undergraduate education. If I were to offer feedback for program improvement, I would highly recommend creating more structured, interdisciplinary networking events where GCSP scholars working on different themes (such as Sustainability and Joy of Living) are tasked to collaborate on micro-projects. This would foster even deeper cross-pollination of ideas and allow scholars to practice their interdisciplinary skills in real-time.
